# Architecture approaches

Use this as a comparison aid, not a catalog to apply wholesale. Most systems need only one or two of these ideas. Several named styles describe compatible ways to organize boundaries rather than competing full-system choices.

| Approach | Core idea | Strengths | Limitations and costs | Fits when |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Simple modular or layered application** | Keep a small number of cohesive modules or layers, such as transport, application logic, and persistence. Keep dependencies understandable. | Low ceremony, easy local development and deployment, familiar testing and debugging. | Boundaries can erode if modules reach across layers or share mutable state; a generic layer can become a dumping ground. | A small or medium application, CRUD API, or team where one deployable and straightforward boundaries meet current needs. A formal pattern may add nothing. |
| **Clean Architecture** | Keep domain rules and use cases independent of outer frameworks and infrastructure; dependencies point inward. | Business rules can be tested and retained while delivery or storage details change. Makes dependency direction explicit. | Extra use-case and mapping layers, interfaces, and indirection can slow simple work; boundaries require discipline. | Domain behavior has meaningful complexity and there is a real need to isolate it from volatile delivery or infrastructure choices. |
| **Hexagonal Architecture (Ports and Adapters)** | Express application capabilities as ports; connect databases, APIs, queues, and user interfaces through adapters. | Makes integration contracts visible; supports replacing or simulating external systems and testing the core without them. | Ports and adapters can multiply into boilerplate if each call gets an abstraction without a change or testing benefit. | Important external integrations, multiple delivery mechanisms, or a need to isolate volatile I/O from core behavior. It can be used selectively inside one module. |
| **Onion Architecture** | Put domain concepts at the center and organize surrounding policies and infrastructure in rings whose dependencies point inward. | Keeps domain policy independent of infrastructure and gives a clear mental model for domain-heavy code. | Ring-by-ring organization can duplicate Clean/Hexagonal ceremony; the metaphor does not itself guarantee sound boundaries. | A rich domain model benefits from an explicit core, especially when infrastructure details should remain replaceable. |
| **Modular Monolith** | Keep a single deployable application while enforcing cohesive modules and explicit dependencies or ownership. | Strong internal boundaries without network calls, distributed deployment, or distributed data management. Easier transactions and local testing than split services. | Modules still share a release and runtime; isolation depends on enforcing boundaries. A poorly modularized monolith remains tightly coupled. | Several business capabilities need clear ownership, but independent deployment, scaling, or fault isolation is not yet worth distributed operations. |
| **Microservices** | Split a system into independently deployable services, typically with service-owned data and explicit network contracts. | Teams can release and scale services independently; failures and technology choices can be isolated at service boundaries. | High cost: network failure, latency, tracing, deployment automation, service discovery, contract/version management, data consistency, on-call, and local development. | Independent team ownership, release cadence, scaling, compliance, or fault isolation is a demonstrated requirement and the organization can operate the system. |
| **Event-driven architecture** | Producers publish events and consumers react asynchronously, often through a broker or durable log. | Decouples timing and allows multiple consumers; useful for background work and fan-out. | Eventual consistency, duplicate delivery, ordering, retries, idempotency, schema evolution, replay, and observability need explicit design. | Workflows are naturally asynchronous, consumers need independent cadence, or durable fan-out is required. It may complement a monolith or services. |
| **CQRS** | Separate write-side behavior from read-side projections or models. | Supports distinct validation and read-optimization needs; can serve complex query shapes or strongly different read/write loads. | More models and synchronization paths, projection lag, rebuild/replay procedures, and harder end-to-end reasoning. | Read and write needs materially differ or complex read projections justify the added consistency and operational work. It does not require microservices or event sourcing. |
| **Serverless** | Run managed functions or application units in response to events or requests, with the provider managing much of the runtime scaling. | Low server-management burden, elastic capacity, and useful pay-per-use behavior for suitable workloads. | Provider/runtime coupling, execution limits, cold starts, local testing and tracing challenges, concurrency limits, and cost surprises under sustained load. | Bursty or event-triggered workloads fit managed platform capabilities and the team accepts its limits and provider dependencies. |

Other approaches—such as a service-based application, pipes and filters, data-oriented design, or client/server—may fit when their defining constraints appear in the requirements. Describe the relevant mechanism and trade-offs directly; do not force a named style where a clear design description is more useful.

## Compare the real costs

For each serious candidate, consider:

- What changes become easier, and which become harder?
- Where do responsibilities and data ownership sit? Can a change cross a boundary without hidden coupling?
- What new runtime failure modes, consistency rules, deployment steps, monitoring, or on-call work appear?
- How does the design affect test setup, integration confidence, local development, and recovery?
- Which existing constraints make this approach practical for this team?
- What concrete signal would justify evolving the design later?

Do not give every candidate equal attention. Discard approaches that do not address a real requirement, and compare the recommended approach with the simplest plausible alternative.
